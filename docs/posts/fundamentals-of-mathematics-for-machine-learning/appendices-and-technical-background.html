Appendices and Technical Background

This appendix provides essential technical results and mathematical foundations that support the main chapters of these tutorials. The material is organized into three main sections: technical lemmas and inequalities used in learning theory proofs (Section 1.1), a comprehensive review of linear algebra concepts (Section 1.2), and fundamental concentration inequalities from probability theory (Section 1.3).

These results are referenced throughout these tutorials, particularly in Foundations of Learning Theory where we establish generalization bounds, and in Supervised Learning where we analyze algorithm convergence. Readers encountering unfamiliar mathematical concepts in the main chapters should consult the relevant sections here for detailed definitions and proofs.

Technical Lemmas and Inequalities

This appendix presents fundamental technical lemmas and inequalities that are frequently used throughout these tutorials. These results provide the mathematical foundation for many of the theoretical guarantees and convergence analyses in machine learning theory.

Basic Algebraic Lemmas

The following lemmas establish relationships between logarithmic and linear functions that are essential for deriving sample complexity bounds.

Lemma A.1 (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let a > 0. Then: x \geq 2a\log(a) \Rightarrow x \geq a\log(x). It follows that a necessary condition for the inequality x < a\log(x) to hold is that x < 2a\log(a).

Proof. First note that for a \in (0, \sqrt{e}] the inequality x \geq a\log(x) holds unconditionally and therefore the claim is trivial. From now on, assume that a > \sqrt{e}. Consider the function f(x) = x - a\log(x). The derivative is f'(x) = 1 - a/x. Thus, for x > a the derivative is positive and the function increases. In addition,

\begin{aligned} f(2a\log(a)) &= 2a\log(a) - a\log(2a\log(a)) \\ &= 2a\log(a) - a\log(a) - a\log(2\log(a)) \\ &= a\log(a) - a\log(2\log(a)) \end{aligned}

Since a - 2\log(a) > 0 for all a > 0, the proof follows. \square

This lemma is particularly useful when analyzing the relationship between sample size and VC dimension in PAC learning bounds.

Lemma A.2 (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let a \geq 1 and b > 0. Then: x \geq 4a\log(2a) + 2b \Rightarrow x \geq a\log(x) + b.

Proof. It suffices to prove that x \geq 4a\log(2a) + 2b implies that both x \geq 2a\log(x) and x \geq 2b. Since we assume a \geq 1 we clearly have that x \geq 2b. In addition, since b > 0 we have that x \geq 4a\log(2a) which using Lemma A.1 implies that x \geq 2a\log(x). This concludes our proof. \square

This result is used to simplify sample complexity expressions in agnostic PAC learning.

Concentration Lemmas

The following lemmas relate tail bounds to expectations, which are crucial for deriving generalization bounds.

Lemma A.3 (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let X be a random variable and x' \in \mathbb{R} be a scalar and assume that there exists a > 0 such that for all t \geq 0 we have \mathbb{P}[|X - x'| > t] \leq 2e^{-t^2/a^2}. Then, \mathbb{E}[|X - x'|] \leq 4a.

Proof. For all i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots denote t_i = ai. Since t_i is monotonically increasing we have that \mathbb{E}[|X - x'|] is at most \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} t_i \mathbb{P}[|X - x'| > t_{i-1}]. Combining this with the assumption in the lemma we get that \mathbb{E}[|X - x'|] \leq 2a\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} ie^{-(i-1)^2}. The proof now follows from the inequalities

\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} ie^{-(i-1)^2} \leq \sum_{i=1}^{5} ie^{-(i-1)^2} + \int_5^{\infty} xe^{-(x-1)^2}dx < 1.8 + 10^{-7} < 2

\square

Lemma A.4 (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let X be a random variable and x' \in \mathbb{R} be a scalar and assume that there exists a > 0 and b \geq e such that for all t \geq 0 we have \mathbb{P}[|X - x'| > t] \leq 2be^{-t^2/a^2}. Then, \mathbb{E}[|X - x'|] \leq a(2 + \sqrt{\log(b)}).

Proof. For all i = 0, 1, 2, \ldots denote t_i = a(i + \sqrt{\log(b)}). Since t_i is monotonically increasing we have that

\mathbb{E}[|X - x'|] \leq a\sqrt{\log(b)} + \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} t_i \mathbb{P}[|X - x'| > t_{i-1}]

Using the assumption in the lemma we have

\begin{aligned} \sum_{i=1}^{\infty} t_i \mathbb{P}[|X - x'| > t_{i-1}] &\leq 2ab\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} (i + \sqrt{\log(b)})e^{-(i-1+\sqrt{\log(b)})^2} \\ &\leq 2ab\int_{1+\sqrt{\log(b)}}^{\infty} xe^{-(x-1)^2}dx \\ &= 2ab\int_{\sqrt{\log(b)}}^{\infty} (y+1)e^{-y^2}dy \\ &\leq 4ab\int_{\sqrt{\log(b)}}^{\infty} ye^{-y^2}dy \\ &= 2ab[-e^{-y^2}]_{\sqrt{\log(b)}}^{\infty} \\ &= 2ab/b = 2a \end{aligned}

Combining the preceding inequalities we conclude our proof. \square

This lemma is essential for analyzing Rademacher complexity and deriving data-dependent generalization bounds.

Combinatorial Lemmas

Lemma A.5 (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let m, d be two positive integers such that d \leq m - 2. Then,

\sum_{k=0}^{d} \binom{m}{k} \leq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d

Proof. We prove the claim by induction. For d = 1 the left-hand side equals 1 + m while the right-hand side equals em; hence the claim is true. Assume that the claim holds for d and let us prove it for d + 1. By the induction assumption we have

\begin{aligned} \sum_{k=0}^{d+1} \binom{m}{k} &\leq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d + \binom{m}{d+1} \\ &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \left(1 + \left(\frac{d}{em}\right)^d \frac{m(m-1)(m-2)\cdots(m-d)}{(d+1)d!}\right) \\ &\leq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \left(1 + \left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^d \frac{(m-d)}{(d+1)d!}\right) \end{aligned}

Using Stirling’s approximation we further have that

\begin{aligned} &\leq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \left(1 + \left(\frac{d}{e}\right)^d \frac{(m-d)}{(d+1)\sqrt{2\pi d}(d/e)^d}\right) \\ &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \left(1 + \frac{m-d}{\sqrt{2\pi d}(d+1)}\right) \\ &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{d+1+(m-d)/\sqrt{2\pi d}}{d+1} \\ &\leq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{d+1+(m-d)/2}{d+1} \\ &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{d/2+1+m/2}{d+1} \\ &\leq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{m}{d+1} \end{aligned}

where in the last inequality we used the assumption that d \leq m - 2. On the other hand,

\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{em}{d+1}\right)^{d+1} &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{em}{d+1} \cdot \left(\frac{d}{d+1}\right)^d \\ &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{em}{d+1} \cdot \frac{1}{(1+1/d)^d} \\ &\geq \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{em}{d+1} \cdot \frac{1}{e} \\ &= \left(\frac{em}{d}\right)^d \cdot \frac{m}{d+1} \end{aligned}

which proves our inductive argument. \square

This lemma provides the key bound on growth functions used in Sauer’s lemma and VC dimension analysis.

Exponential Inequalities

Lemma A.6 (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). For all a \in \mathbb{R} we have

\frac{e^a + e^{-a}}{2} \leq e^{a^2/2}

Proof. Observe that

e^a = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{a^n}{n!}

Therefore,

\frac{e^a + e^{-a}}{2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{a^{2n}}{(2n)!}

and

e^{a^2/2} = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} \frac{a^{2n}}{2^n n!}

Observing that (2n)! \geq 2^n n! for every n \geq 0 we conclude our proof. \square

This inequality is fundamental in deriving Hoeffding’s inequality and other concentration bounds.

Linear Algebra Recap

This section provides a comprehensive review of linear algebra concepts that are essential for understanding machine learning algorithms and their theoretical foundations. We focus on finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces and present key definitions, theorems, and decompositions. These results complement the linear algebra introduction in Linear Algebra and Geometry and provide the technical details needed for understanding matrix decompositions, optimization algorithms, and dimensionality reduction techniques discussed in Supervised Learning and Unsupervised Learning.

For more on this topic, see Zhao (2025, Appendix).

Basic Definitions and Notation

In these tutorials, we work exclusively with linear algebra over finite-dimensional Euclidean spaces. Vectors are treated as column vectors unless otherwise specified.

Inner Products and Norms. Given two d-dimensional vectors \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^d, their inner product is defined as

\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v} \rangle = \sum_{i=1}^{d} u_i v_i

The Euclidean norm (also called the \ell_2 norm) is \|\mathbf{u}\| = \sqrt{\langle \mathbf{u}, \mathbf{u} \rangle}. We also frequently use the \ell_1 norm, \|\mathbf{u}\|_1 = \sum_{i=1}^{d} |u_i|, and the \ell_{\infty} norm, \|\mathbf{u}\|_{\infty} = \max_i |u_i|.

Subspaces and Span. A subspace of \mathbb{R}^d is a subset of \mathbb{R}^d that is closed under addition and scalar multiplication. The span of a set of vectors \mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k is the subspace containing all vectors of the form

\sum_{i=1}^{k} \alpha_i \mathbf{u}_i

where for all i, \alpha_i \in \mathbb{R}.

Linear Independence and Bases. A set of vectors U = \{\mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k\} is independent if for every i, \mathbf{u}_i is not in the span of \mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_{i-1}, \mathbf{u}_{i+1}, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_k. We say that U spans a subspace V if V is the span of the vectors in U. We say that U is a basis of V if it is both independent and spans V. The dimension of V is the size of a basis of V, and it can be verified that all bases of V have the same size (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014).

Orthogonality. We say that U is an orthogonal set if for all i \neq j, \langle \mathbf{u}_i, \mathbf{u}_j \rangle = 0. We say that U is an orthonormal set if it is orthogonal and if for every i, \|\mathbf{u}_i\| = 1.

Matrix Properties. Given a matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}, the range of A is the span of its columns and the null space of A is the subspace of all vectors that satisfy A\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{0}. The rank of A is the dimension of its range.

The transpose of a matrix A, denoted A^{\top}, is the matrix whose (i,j) entry equals the (j,i) entry of A. We say that A is symmetric if A = A^{\top}.

Eigenvalues and Eigenvectors

Eigenvalue decomposition is fundamental to understanding many machine learning algorithms, including principal component analysis and spectral clustering.

Definition. Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} be a matrix. A nonzero vector \mathbf{u} is an eigenvector of A with a corresponding eigenvalue \lambda if

A\mathbf{u} = \lambda\mathbf{u}

Theorem (Spectral Decomposition) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). If A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} is a symmetric matrix of rank k, then there exists an orthonormal basis of \mathbb{R}^d, \mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_d, such that each \mathbf{u}_i is an eigenvector of A. Furthermore, A can be written as

A = \sum_{i=1}^{d} \lambda_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{u}_i^{\top}

where each \lambda_i is the eigenvalue corresponding to the eigenvector \mathbf{u}_i. This can be written equivalently as A = UDU^{\top}, where the columns of U are the vectors \mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_d, and D is a diagonal matrix with D_{i,i} = \lambda_i and for i \neq j, D_{i,j} = 0. Finally, the number of \lambda_i which are nonzero is the rank of the matrix, the eigenvectors which correspond to the nonzero eigenvalues span the range of A, and the eigenvectors which correspond to zero eigenvalues span the null space of A.

This decomposition is central to understanding the geometry of quadratic forms and optimization landscapes in machine learning.

Positive Definite Matrices

Positive definite matrices play a crucial role in optimization theory and kernel methods.

Definition. A symmetric matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} is positive definite if all its eigenvalues are positive. A is positive semidefinite if all its eigenvalues are nonnegative.

Theorem (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} be a symmetric matrix. Then, the following are equivalent definitions of positive semidefiniteness of A:

  1. All the eigenvalues of A are nonnegative. 2. For every vector \mathbf{u}, \langle \mathbf{u}, A\mathbf{u} \rangle \geq 0.
  2. There exists a matrix B such that A = BB^{\top}.

The second characterization is particularly useful in optimization, as it ensures that quadratic forms are convex. The third characterization connects positive semidefiniteness to the existence of matrix factorizations, which is fundamental in kernel methods and Gaussian processes.

Singular Value Decomposition

The Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) generalizes eigenvalue decomposition to non-square matrices and is fundamental to dimensionality reduction techniques.

Definition. Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} be a matrix of rank r. Unit vectors \mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n and \mathbf{u} \in \mathbb{R}^m are called right and left singular vectors of A with corresponding singular value \sigma > 0 if

A\mathbf{v} = \sigma\mathbf{u} \quad \text{and} \quad A^{\top}\mathbf{u} = \sigma\mathbf{v}

Lemma (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} be a matrix of rank r. Assume that \mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_r is an orthonormal set of right singular vectors of A, \mathbf{u}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{u}_r is an orthonormal set of corresponding left singular vectors of A, and \sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_r are the corresponding singular values. Then,

A = \sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i \mathbf{u}_i \mathbf{v}_i^{\top}

It follows that if U is a matrix whose columns are the \mathbf{u}_i’s, V is a matrix whose columns are the \mathbf{v}_i’s, and D is a diagonal matrix with D_{i,i} = \sigma_i, then

A = UDV^{\top}

Connection to Eigenvalues. The singular values of A are intimately related to the eigenvalues of A^{\top}A and AA^{\top}.

Lemma (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{u} are right and left singular vectors of A with singular value \sigma if and only if \mathbf{v} is an eigenvector of A^{\top}A with corresponding eigenvalue \sigma^2 and \mathbf{u} = \sigma^{-1}A\mathbf{v} is an eigenvector of AA^{\top} with corresponding eigenvalue \sigma^2.

Construction of Singular Vectors. The following result shows how to construct singular vectors through optimization.

Lemma (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} with rank r. Define the following vectors:

\begin{aligned} \mathbf{v}_1 &= \arg\max_{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n: \|\mathbf{v}\| = 1} \|A\mathbf{v}\| \\ \mathbf{v}_2 &= \arg\max_{\substack{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n: \|\mathbf{v}\| = 1 \\ \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v}_1 \rangle = 0}} \|A\mathbf{v}\| \\ &\vdots \\ \mathbf{v}_r &= \arg\max_{\substack{\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^n: \|\mathbf{v}\| = 1 \\ \forall i < r, \langle \mathbf{v}, \mathbf{v}_i \rangle = 0}} \|A\mathbf{v}\| \end{aligned}

Then, \mathbf{v}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{v}_r is an orthonormal set of right singular vectors of A.

This construction provides the foundation for principal component analysis, where we seek directions of maximum variance in data.

Theorem (SVD Theorem) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} with rank r. Then A = UDV^{\top} where D is an r \times r matrix with nonzero singular values of A and the columns of U, V are orthonormal left and right singular vectors of A. Furthermore, for all i, D_{i,i}^2 is an eigenvalue of A^{\top}A, the i-th column of V is the corresponding eigenvector of A^{\top}A, and the i-th column of U is the corresponding eigenvector of AA^{\top}.

The SVD is fundamental to many machine learning techniques including:

  • Principal Component Analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction - Low-rank matrix approximation and matrix completion - Latent semantic analysis in natural language processing - Recommender systems based on collaborative filtering - Image compression and denoising

The optimality properties of SVD make it a cornerstone of modern data analysis and machine learning.

Additional Linear Algebra Topics

Matrix Norms and Operator Norms. For matrices, we can define various norms. The Frobenius norm of a matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n} is defined as

\|A\|_F = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{m} \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij}^2}

The operator norm (or spectral norm) is defined as

\|A\|_2 = \max_{\|\mathbf{x}\|=1} \|A\mathbf{x}\|

which equals the largest singular value of A (Deisenroth et al. 2020).

Trace and Determinant. The trace of a square matrix A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n} is the sum of its diagonal elements:

\text{tr}(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ii}

The trace has the cyclic property: \text{tr}(ABC) = \text{tr}(CAB) = \text{tr}(BCA). The determinant of A is the product of its eigenvalues and provides information about the volume scaling induced by the linear transformation.

These linear algebra concepts form the mathematical foundation for understanding optimization algorithms, dimensionality reduction techniques, and the geometry of high-dimensional spaces in machine learning.

Measure Concentration and Probability Inequalities

Measure concentration inequalities quantify the deviation of empirical averages from their expectations when the sample size is finite. These inequalities are fundamental to statistical learning theory and provide the basis for generalization bounds in machine learning (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014; Zhao 2025). The results in this section are used extensively in Foundations of Learning Theory to derive PAC learning bounds and in Supervised Learning to analyze the convergence of learning algorithms.

Let Z_1, \ldots, Z_m be an i.i.d. sequence of random variables and let \mu be their mean. The strong law of large numbers states that when m tends to infinity, the empirical average, \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i, converges to the expected value \mu, with probability 1. Measure concentration inequalities quantify this deviation for finite m.

Markov’s Inequality

We start with Markov’s inequality, which provides a basic bound for nonnegative random variables. Let Z be a nonnegative random variable. The expectation of Z can be written as:

\mathbb{E}[Z] = \int_{x=0}^{\infty} \mathbb{P}[Z \geq x]dx

Since \mathbb{P}[Z \geq x] is monotonically nonincreasing we obtain

\forall a \geq 0, \quad \mathbb{E}[Z] \geq \int_{x=0}^{a} \mathbb{P}[Z \geq x]dx \geq \int_{x=0}^{a} \mathbb{P}[Z \geq a]dx = a\mathbb{P}[Z \geq a]

Rearranging the inequality yields Markov’s inequality:

\forall a \geq 0, \quad \mathbb{P}[Z \geq a] \leq \frac{\mathbb{E}[Z]}{a}

Lemma (Markov’s Inequality for Bounded Variables) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let Z be a random variable that takes values in [0,1]. Assume that \mathbb{E}[Z] = \mu. Then, for any a \in (0,1),

\mathbb{P}[Z > 1-a] \geq \frac{\mu - (1-a)}{a}

This also implies that for every a \in (0,1),

\mathbb{P}[Z > a] \geq \frac{\mu - a}{1-a} \geq \mu - a

Chebyshev’s Inequality

Applying Markov’s inequality on the random variable (Z - \mathbb{E}[Z])^2 we obtain Chebyshev’s inequality:

\forall a > 0, \quad \mathbb{P}[|Z - \mathbb{E}[Z]| \geq a] = \mathbb{P}[(Z - \mathbb{E}[Z])^2 \geq a^2] \leq \frac{\text{Var}[Z]}{a^2}

where \text{Var}[Z] = \mathbb{E}[(Z - \mathbb{E}[Z])^2] is the variance of Z.

Consider the random variable \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i. Since Z_1, \ldots, Z_m are i.i.d. it is easy to verify that

\text{Var}\left[\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i\right] = \frac{\text{Var}[Z_1]}{m}

Lemma (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let Z_1, \ldots, Z_m be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables and assume that \mathbb{E}[Z_1] = \mu and \text{Var}[Z_1] \leq 1. Then, for any \delta \in (0,1), with probability of at least 1-\delta we have

\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i - \mu\right| \leq \sqrt{\frac{1}{\delta m}}

The deviation between the empirical average and the mean given by Chebyshev’s inequality decreases polynomially with m. It is possible to obtain significantly faster, exponential decrease using Chernoff’s bounds and Hoeffding’s inequality.

Chernoff’s Bounds

Chernoff’s bounds provide exponentially decreasing tail probabilities for sums of independent Bernoulli random variables.

Lemma (Chernoff Bound - Upper Tail) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let Z_1, \ldots, Z_m be independent Bernoulli variables where for every i, \mathbb{P}[Z_i = 1] = p_i and \mathbb{P}[Z_i = 0] = 1 - p_i. Let p = \sum_{i=1}^{m} p_i and let Z = \sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i. Then, for any \delta > 0,

\mathbb{P}[Z > (1+\delta)p] \leq e^{-h(\delta)p}

where

h(\delta) = (1+\delta)\log(1+\delta) - \delta

Using the inequality h(a) \geq a^2/(2 + 2a/3) we obtain the simplified bound:

\mathbb{P}[Z > (1+\delta)p] \leq e^{-p\frac{\delta^2}{2+2\delta/3}}

Lemma (Chernoff Bound - Lower Tail) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Using the notation above, we also have

\mathbb{P}[Z < (1-\delta)p] \leq e^{-ph(-\delta)} \leq e^{-ph(\delta)} \leq e^{-p\frac{\delta^2}{2+2\delta/3}}

These bounds show that the probability of large deviations decreases exponentially with the sample size, which is much faster than the polynomial rate given by Chebyshev’s inequality.

Hoeffding’s Inequality

Hoeffding’s inequality generalizes Chernoff’s bounds to arbitrary bounded random variables, not just Bernoulli variables.

Lemma (Hoeffding’s Inequality) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let Z_1, \ldots, Z_m be a sequence of i.i.d. random variables and let \bar{Z} = \frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i. Assume that \mathbb{E}[\bar{Z}] = \mu and \mathbb{P}[a \leq Z_i \leq b] = 1 for every i. Then, for any \epsilon > 0

\mathbb{P}\left[\left|\frac{1}{m}\sum_{i=1}^{m} Z_i - \mu\right| > \epsilon\right] \leq 2\exp\left(-\frac{2m\epsilon^2}{(b-a)^2}\right)

This inequality is one of the most important tools in statistical learning theory. It provides a uniform bound that holds for all \epsilon > 0 and shows that the probability of deviation decreases exponentially with both the sample size m and the square of the deviation \epsilon^2.

Lemma (Hoeffding’s Lemma) (Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014). Let X be a random variable that takes values in the interval [a,b] and such that \mathbb{E}[X] = 0. Then, for every \lambda > 0,

\mathbb{E}[e^{\lambda X}] \leq e^{\frac{\lambda^2(b-a)^2}{8}}

This technical lemma is the key ingredient in proving Hoeffding’s inequality and demonstrates how moment generating functions can be bounded for bounded random variables.

Applications to Learning Theory

These concentration inequalities have direct applications in learning theory:

  • Uniform Convergence: Hoeffding’s inequality combined with union bounds provides uniform convergence guarantees for finite hypothesis classes. - Sample Complexity: The exponential decay rate determines how many samples are needed to achieve a desired accuracy with high probability. - Generalization Bounds: These inequalities bound the difference between training error and test error, providing theoretical guarantees for learning algorithms.

The transition from polynomial (Chebyshev) to exponential (Hoeffding) concentration is crucial for obtaining practical sample complexity bounds in PAC learning.
