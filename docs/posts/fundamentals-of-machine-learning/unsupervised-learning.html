Unsupervised Learning

Unsupervised learning addresses a fundamental challenge: discovering structure in data without the guidance of labels. Unlike supervised learning (Supervised Learning) where we have input-output pairs, unsupervised learning works with inputs alone, seeking to uncover hidden patterns, groupings, or low-dimensional representations. This paradigm is crucial in practice because labeled data is often expensive or impossible to obtain, while unlabeled data is abundant.

The methods in this chapter serve different purposes: clustering (Section 1.1) groups similar data points together, dimensionality reduction (1) finds compact representations that preserve essential structure, and generative models (Section 1.3) learn probability distributions that can generate new data. These techniques are foundational for exploratory data analysis, data preprocessing, and understanding complex datasets.

Clustering Methods

Clustering is the task of partitioning data into groups (clusters) such that points within a cluster are more similar to each other than to points in other clusters. This is one of the most fundamental problems in unsupervised learning, with applications ranging from customer segmentation in marketing to gene expression analysis in biology.

For more on this topic, see James et al. (2023, Ch. 12) and Theodoridis (2026, Ch. 12).

The challenge is that “similarity” is subjective and problem-dependent. Different clustering algorithms make different assumptions about cluster structure, leading to different solutions. Understanding these assumptions is crucial for choosing the right algorithm.

K-Means Clustering

K-means is the workhorse of clustering algorithms. Developed in the 1950s, it remains one of the most widely used methods due to its simplicity, efficiency, and effectiveness. The algorithm’s elegance lies in its iterative refinement: alternately assign points to clusters and update cluster centers, guaranteed to converge to a local optimum.

Problem Formulation: Given a dataset \mathcal{X} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n\} where \mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d, we want to partition the data into K clusters. We represent this partition by: - Cluster centers \{\boldsymbol{\mu}_1, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_K\} where \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d - Assignments \{c_1, \ldots, c_n\} where c_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\} indicates which cluster contains \mathbf{x}_i

Objective Function: K-means minimizes the within-cluster sum of squares (WCSS), also called inertia or distortion:

Definition 1 K-Means Objective Function

J(\mathbf{C}, \boldsymbol{\mu}) = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in C_k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2 = \sum_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{c_i}\|^2

where C_k = \{i : c_i = k\} is the set of indices of points assigned to cluster k. This objective measures cluster compactness: we want points close to their cluster centers.

Intuition: Imagine you have customer data and want to identify K=3 market segments. K-means finds three “prototype” customers (the centers \boldsymbol{\mu}_k) and assigns each actual customer to their nearest prototype. The objective ensures that customers within a segment are similar (close to the prototype) while segments are distinct.

Lloyd’s Algorithm (Standard K-Means):

The algorithm alternates between two steps that each decrease the objective:

  1. Initialization: Choose K initial cluster centers \boldsymbol{\mu}_1^{(0)}, \ldots, \boldsymbol{\mu}_K^{(0)} (initialization strategies discussed below)

  2. Repeat until convergence:

    1. Assignment Step: Assign each point to the nearest center: c_i^{(t)} = \arg\min_{k \in \{1,\ldots,K\}} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t)}\|^2

    This is optimal for fixed centers: each point goes to its closest center, minimizing its contribution to the objective.

    1. Update Step: Recompute cluster centers as the mean of assigned points: \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{1}{|C_k^{(t)}|}\sum_{i \in C_k^{(t)}} \mathbf{x}_i

    This is optimal for fixed assignments: the mean minimizes the sum of squared distances (see proof below).

  3. Convergence: Stop when assignments no longer change or centers move less than a threshold \epsilon.

Example: Consider 2D data with three natural clusters. Starting with random centers, the algorithm iteratively refines them: - Iteration 1: Centers are random, assignments are poor - Iteration 2: Centers move toward cluster means, assignments improve - Iteration 3-5: Centers converge to cluster centroids - Convergence: Assignments stabilize, centers no longer move

Convergence Properties:

  1. Monotonic Decrease: Each iteration decreases (or maintains) the objective J^{(t+1)} \leq J^{(t)} because: - Assignment step: Choosing the nearest center minimizes distance for each point - Update step: The mean minimizes sum of squared distances (proven below)

  2. Finite Convergence: Since there are finitely many possible assignments (K^n possibilities) and the objective strictly decreases except at convergence, the algorithm must terminate in finite time.

  3. Local Minimum: K-means converges to a local minimum, not necessarily the global optimum. The solution depends critically on initialization, which is why multiple runs with different initializations are recommended.

Proof of Update Step Optimality: For fixed assignments, the optimal center for cluster k minimizes:

\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^* = \arg\min_{\boldsymbol{\mu}} \sum_{i \in C_k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}\|^2

Taking the derivative with respect to \boldsymbol{\mu} and setting to zero:

\frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}} \sum_{i \in C_k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}\|^2 = \frac{\partial}{\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}} \sum_{i \in C_k} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu})^T(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = -2\sum_{i \in C_k} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}) = 0

Solving for \boldsymbol{\mu}: \sum_{i \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_i = |C_k|\boldsymbol{\mu} \implies \boldsymbol{\mu}^* = \frac{1}{|C_k|}\sum_{i \in C_k} \mathbf{x}_i

This is the sample mean of the cluster, justifying the name “K-means.”

Initialization Methods:

  1. Random Initialization: Select K random points as initial centers - Simple but can lead to poor solutions - Highly sensitive to outliers

  2. Forgy Method: Randomly assign points to clusters, then compute initial centers

  3. Random Partition: Randomly partition data, compute centers

  4. K-Means++ (Recommended): Probabilistic initialization that spreads out initial centers:

    1. Choose first center \boldsymbol{\mu}_1 uniformly at random from data

    2. For k = 2, \ldots, K: - For each point \mathbf{x}_i, compute distance to nearest existing center: D(\mathbf{x}_i) = \min_{j < k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|^2

  • Choose next center \boldsymbol{\mu}_k with probability proportional to D(\mathbf{x}_i)^2

    Guarantee: K-means++ initialization ensures that the objective is O(\log K)-competitive with the optimal clustering in expectation.

Computational Complexity:

  • Per iteration: O(nKd) for assignment step, O(nd) for update step - Total: O(nKdT) where T is the number of iterations - Typical behavior: T is often small (10-100), but can be large for difficult datasets - Space: O(nd + Kd) to store data and centers

Choosing the Number of Clusters K:

  1. Elbow Method: Plot WCSS vs. K and look for an “elbow” where the rate of decrease sharply changes: \text{WCSS}(K) = \sum_{k=1}^K \sum_{i \in C_k} \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\|^2 The elbow indicates a good tradeoff between fit and complexity.

  2. Silhouette Analysis: For each point i, compute: s_i = \frac{b_i - a_i}{\max(a_i, b_i)} where: - a_i: Average distance to points in same cluster - b_i: Average distance to points in nearest other cluster

    The silhouette coefficient ranges from -1 to 1: - s_i \approx 1: Well clustered - s_i \approx 0: On cluster boundary - s_i < 0: Possibly misclassified

    Choose K that maximizes average silhouette score.

  3. Gap Statistic: Compare WCSS to expected WCSS under null reference distribution: \text{Gap}(K) = \mathbb{E}[\log(\text{WCSS}_0(K))] - \log(\text{WCSS}(K)) Choose smallest K such that \text{Gap}(K) \geq \text{Gap}(K+1) - s_{K+1} where s_{K+1} is the standard error.

  4. Cross-Validation: Use clustering quality metrics on held-out data.

Assumptions and Limitations:

  1. Spherical Clusters: K-means assumes clusters are roughly spherical (isotropic Gaussian) 2. Similar Sizes: Works best when clusters have similar numbers of points 3. Similar Variances: Assumes clusters have similar spread 4. Convex Boundaries: Cannot capture non-convex cluster shapes 5. Sensitive to Outliers: Outliers can significantly affect cluster centers 6. Euclidean Distance: Assumes Euclidean distance is appropriate

Variants and Extensions:

  1. K-Medoids (PAM): Use actual data points as centers instead of means - More robust to outliers - Works with arbitrary distance metrics - More computationally expensive

  2. K-Means with Different Distances: Generalize to other distance metrics - Manhattan distance: K-medians - Cosine distance: Spherical K-means

  3. Fuzzy C-Means: Soft assignments with membership degrees: u_{ik} = \frac{1}{\sum_{j=1}^K (\|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k\| / \|\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|)^{2/(m-1)}} where m > 1 is the fuzziness parameter.

  4. Mini-Batch K-Means: Use random mini-batches for scalability: - Much faster for large datasets - Slightly lower quality - O(nKd) complexity per epoch

  5. K-Means++: Improved initialization (described above)

  6. X-Means: Automatically determines K by splitting clusters

Practical Considerations:

  1. Feature Scaling: Standardize features to have zero mean and unit variance: \mathbf{x}_i' = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}} Otherwise, features with larger scales dominate.

  2. Multiple Runs: Run K-means multiple times with different initializations and choose the best result (lowest WCSS).

  3. Convergence Criteria: Stop when: - Assignments don’t change - Centers move less than threshold: \max_k \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t)}\| < \epsilon

  • Maximum iterations reached

  1. Empty Clusters: If a cluster becomes empty: - Reinitialize its center - Split the largest cluster - Remove the empty cluster

  2. High-Dimensional Data: K-means can struggle in high dimensions due to the curse of dimensionality. Consider dimensionality reduction first.

Applications: - Image compression (color quantization) - Customer segmentation - Document clustering - Anomaly detection - Feature learning (as preprocessing) - Vector quantization

K-means remains one of the most practical and widely used clustering algorithms despite its limitations, particularly when combined with good initialization and appropriate preprocessing (James et al. 2023; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014).

Hierarchical Clustering

Hierarchical clustering builds a hierarchy of clusters, producing a tree structure (dendrogram) that represents nested groupings at different scales. Unlike K-means (see Section 1.1), hierarchical methods don’t require specifying the number of clusters in advance and provide a complete clustering hierarchy.

Two Approaches:

  1. Agglomerative (Bottom-Up): Start with individual points and merge clusters 2. Divisive (Top-Down): Start with all points in one cluster and recursively split

Agglomerative methods are more common and computationally tractable.

Agglomerative Hierarchical Clustering Algorithm:

``` Input: Data points {x₁, …, xₙ}, distance metric d, linkage criterion L Initialize: Each point as its own cluster: C = {{x₁}, {x₂}, …, {xₙ}}

While |C| > 1: Find pair of closest clusters: (Cᵢ, Cⱼ) = argmin_{Cₐ,Cᵦ∈C, a≠b} L(Cₐ, Cᵦ) Merge clusters: C_new = Cᵢ ∪ Cⱼ C = (C
{Cᵢ, Cⱼ}) ∪ {C_new} Record merge in dendrogram

Return: Dendrogram encoding merge sequence ```

Linkage Criteria: The linkage criterion L(C_i, C_j) defines the distance between clusters:

1. Single Linkage (Minimum Linkage): d_{\text{single}}(C_i, C_j) = \min_{\mathbf{x} \in C_i, \mathbf{x}' \in C_j} d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')

Distance between closest points in the two clusters.

Properties: - Can find non-convex clusters - Sensitive to noise and outliers (chaining effect) - Tends to produce elongated clusters - Computationally efficient

2. Complete Linkage (Maximum Linkage): d_{\text{complete}}(C_i, C_j) = \max_{\mathbf{x} \in C_i, \mathbf{x}' \in C_j} d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')

Distance between farthest points in the two clusters.

Properties: - Produces compact, spherical clusters - Less sensitive to outliers than single linkage - Breaks large clusters - Preferred for well-separated clusters

3. Average Linkage (UPGMA): d_{\text{average}}(C_i, C_j) = \frac{1}{|C_i||C_j|}\sum_{\mathbf{x} \in C_i}\sum_{\mathbf{x}' \in C_j} d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}')

Average distance between all pairs of points.

Properties: - Compromise between single and complete linkage - Less sensitive to outliers - Produces relatively balanced clusters - Widely used in practice

4. Centroid Linkage: d_{\text{centroid}}(C_i, C_j) = d(\boldsymbol{\mu}_i, \boldsymbol{\mu}_j)

where \boldsymbol{\mu}_i = \frac{1}{|C_i|}\sum_{\mathbf{x} \in C_i} \mathbf{x} is the centroid.

Properties: - Intuitive interpretation - Can produce inversions in dendrogram - Similar to K-means distance (see Section 1.1)

5. Ward’s Method (Minimum Variance):

Minimize increase in total within-cluster variance: d_{\text{Ward}}(C_i, C_j) = \frac{|C_i||C_j|}{|C_i| + |C_j|} \|\boldsymbol{\mu}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|^2

Equivalently, minimize: \Delta(C_i, C_j) = \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in C_i \cup C_j} \|\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_{ij}\|^2 - \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in C_i} \|\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_i\|^2 - \sum_{\mathbf{x} \in C_j} \|\mathbf{x} - \boldsymbol{\mu}_j\|^2

where \boldsymbol{\mu}_{ij} is the centroid of the merged cluster.

Properties: - Produces compact, spherical clusters - Minimizes same objective as K-means - Most popular linkage method - Tends to create equal-sized clusters

Dendrograms: A dendrogram is a tree diagram that records the sequence of merges:

  • Vertical axis: Distance or dissimilarity at which clusters merge - Horizontal axis: Individual data points - Branches: Connect clusters that merge - Height: Indicates merge distance

Cutting the Dendrogram: To obtain k clusters, cut the dendrogram at a height that produces k branches:

  1. Fixed number of clusters: Cut to produce exactly k clusters 2. Distance threshold: Cut at height h to get clusters with maximum within-cluster distance \leq h
  2. Inconsistency criterion: Cut where merge distances increase sharply

Computational Complexity:

  • Naive implementation: O(n^3) time, O(n^2) space - O(n^2) to compute all pairwise distances - O(n) iterations, each requiring O(n^2) to find minimum

  • Efficient implementation (SLINK for single linkage): O(n^2) time

  • Space: O(n^2) to store distance matrix

Lance-Williams Update Formula: For efficient computation, update distances after each merge:

d(C_k, C_i \cup C_j) = \alpha_i d(C_k, C_i) + \alpha_j d(C_k, C_j) + \beta d(C_i, C_j) + \gamma |d(C_k, C_i) - d(C_k, C_j)|

Different linkage methods correspond to different values of \alpha_i, \alpha_j, \beta, \gamma:

  • Single: \alpha_i = \alpha_j = 0.5, \beta = 0, \gamma = -0.5
  • Complete: \alpha_i = \alpha_j = 0.5, \beta = 0, \gamma = 0.5
  • Average: \alpha_i = |C_i|/(|C_i|+|C_j|), \alpha_j = |C_j|/(|C_i|+|C_j|), \beta = \gamma = 0
  • Ward’s: More complex formula involving cluster sizes

Advantages of Hierarchical Clustering:

  1. No need to specify k: Produces complete hierarchy 2. Deterministic: No random initialization (unlike K-means) 3. Dendrogram visualization: Provides insight into data structure 4. Flexible: Can cut at any level to get different granularities 5. Works with any distance metric: Not limited to Euclidean distance

Disadvantages:

  1. Computational cost: O(n^2) or O(n^3) time and O(n^2) space 2. Not scalable: Impractical for large datasets (n > 10,000) 3. Greedy: Merges are irreversible, no global optimization 4. Sensitive to noise: Especially single linkage 5. Difficulty with high dimensions: Distance metrics become less meaningful

Comparison with K-Means:

Aspect | K-Means | Hierarchical | |——–|———|————-| | Number of clusters | Must specify | Flexible | |

Complexity | O(nkdT) | O(n^2) to O(n^3) | | Scalability | Good | Poor | | Cluster shape | Spherical | Flexible (depends on linkage) | | Deterministic | No (random init) | Yes | | Visualization | Limited | Dendrogram |

Practical Considerations:

  1. Distance metric: Choice significantly affects results - Euclidean: For continuous features - Manhattan: For mixed features

    • Cosine: For text data - Correlation: For time series

  2. Feature scaling: Standardize features to equal importance (see Linear Algebra and Geometry for normalization)

  3. Linkage selection: - Ward’s: Default choice for most applications - Complete: When clusters are well-separated - Average: Compromise between single and complete - Single: For non-convex clusters (use with caution)

  4. Determining number of clusters: - Look for large gaps in dendrogram heights - Use silhouette analysis - Domain knowledge

  5. Handling large datasets: - Sample data first - Use mini-batch hierarchical clustering - Consider BIRCH (Balanced Iterative Reducing and Clustering using Hierarchies)

Applications:

  • Taxonomy construction in biology - Document organization - Gene expression analysis - Social network analysis - Image segmentation

Hierarchical clustering provides a rich representation of data structure at multiple scales, complementing partition-based methods like K-means (James et al. 2023; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014).

Density-Based Clustering

Density-based clustering identifies clusters as regions of high density separated by regions of low density. Unlike K-means (see Section 1.1) which assumes spherical clusters, density-based methods can discover clusters of arbitrary shape and automatically identify outliers.

DBSCAN (Density-Based Spatial Clustering of Applications with Noise):

DBSCAN is the most widely used density-based clustering algorithm. It groups together points that are closely packed while marking points in low-density regions as outliers.

Key Definitions:

  1. \epsilon-neighborhood: The set of points within distance \epsilon of a point \mathbf{x}: N_\epsilon(\mathbf{x}) = \{\mathbf{x}' \in \mathcal{X} : d(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') \leq \epsilon\}

  2. Core point: A point with at least MinPts neighbors in its \epsilon-neighborhood: |N_\epsilon(\mathbf{x})| \geq \text{MinPts} Core points are in dense regions.

  3. Border point: A point that is not a core point but lies in the \epsilon-neighborhood of a core point. Border points are on the edge of clusters.

  4. Noise point (Outlier): A point that is neither a core point nor a border point.

  5. Directly density-reachable: Point \mathbf{x}' is directly density-reachable from \mathbf{x} if: - \mathbf{x}' is in N_\epsilon(\mathbf{x})

  • \mathbf{x} is a core point

    Note: This relation is not symmetric.

  1. Density-reachable: Point \mathbf{x}' is density-reachable from \mathbf{x} if there exists a chain of points \mathbf{p}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{p}_n where \mathbf{p}_1 = \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{p}_n = \mathbf{x}', and each \mathbf{p}_{i+1} is directly density-reachable from \mathbf{p}_i.

  2. Density-connected: Points \mathbf{x} and \mathbf{x}' are density-connected if there exists a point \mathbf{o} such that both \mathbf{x} and \mathbf{x}' are density-reachable from \mathbf{o}. This relation is symmetric.

Cluster Definition: A cluster C satisfies: 1. Maximality: If \mathbf{x} \in C and \mathbf{x}' is density-reachable from \mathbf{x}, then \mathbf{x}' \in C 2. Connectivity: All points in C are density-connected

DBSCAN Algorithm:

``` Input: Dataset X, parameters ε, MinPts Output: Cluster assignments

Initialize: All points as unvisited Cluster_ID = 0

For each unvisited point x in X: Mark x as visited N = get_neighbors(x, ε) // Find ε-neighborhood

If |N| < MinPts: Mark x as noise Else: Cluster_ID = Cluster_ID + 1 expand_cluster(x, N, Cluster_ID, ε, MinPts)

Function expand_cluster(x, N, Cluster_ID, ε, MinPts): Add x to Cluster_ID

For each point x' in N: If x' is unvisited: Mark x' as visited N' = get_neighbors(x', ε) If |N'| ≥ MinPts: N = N ∪ N'  //
    Expand neighborhood
    
    If x' is not yet in any cluster: Add x' to Cluster_ID ```

Computational Complexity:

  • Without spatial index: O(n^2) time - Each point requires checking all other points

  • **With spatial index (R*-tree, KD-tree):** O(n \log n) average case - Neighborhood queries become O(\log n)

  • Space: O(n) for storing cluster assignments

Parameter Selection:

Choosing \epsilon and MinPts is crucial:

1. MinPts: - Rule of thumb: MinPts \geq d + 1 where d is the dimensionality - Common choice: MinPts = 4 for 2D data, MinPts = 5-10 for higher dimensions - Larger MinPts: More robust to noise, but may merge clusters - Smaller MinPts: More sensitive to noise, may split clusters

2. \epsilon (k-distance graph method):

  1. For each point, compute distance to its k-th nearest neighbor (use k = MinPts) 2. Sort these distances in ascending order
  2. Plot the sorted k-distances 4. Look for an “elbow” or sharp change in the curve 5. Choose \epsilon at the elbow point

Intuition: The elbow indicates the transition from dense regions (small k-distances) to sparse regions (large k-distances).

Advantages of DBSCAN:

  1. Arbitrary cluster shapes: Can find non-convex clusters (unlike K-means) 2. Automatic outlier detection: Identifies noise points naturally 3. No need to specify number of clusters: Determined automatically 4. Robust to outliers: Outliers don’t affect cluster formation 5. Single scan: Only requires one pass through data 6. Deterministic: Same result every time (no random initialization)

Disadvantages:

  1. Parameter sensitivity: Results depend heavily on \epsilon and MinPts 2. Varying densities: Struggles with clusters of different densities 3. High-dimensional data: Distance becomes less meaningful (curse of dimensionality, see
    1. Border point ambiguity: Border points may be assigned to different clusters 5. Memory requirements: Spatial index can be memory-intensive

Variants and Extensions:

1. HDBSCAN (Hierarchical DBSCAN): - Builds hierarchy of clusters at different density levels - Automatically selects optimal \epsilon for each cluster - More robust to varying densities - Uses minimum spanning tree approach

2. OPTICS (Ordering Points To Identify Clustering Structure): - Produces ordering of points representing density-based clustering structure - Creates reachability plot showing cluster hierarchy - Doesn’t require explicit \epsilon parameter - Can extract clusters at multiple scales

3. DENCLUE (DENsity-based CLUstEring): - Uses kernel density estimation - Models clusters as density attractors - Can handle arbitrary shapes and noise

Comparison with Other Methods:

Aspect | DBSCAN | K-Means | Hierarchical | |——–|——–|———|————-| | Cluster shape | Arbitrary | Spherical |

Depends on linkage | | Number of clusters | Automatic | Must specify | Flexible | | Outlier handling | Explicit | Assigns all | Assigns all | | Complexity | O(n \log n) | O(nkdT) | O(n^2) | | Parameters | \epsilon, MinPts | k | Linkage, cut height | | Varying density | Poor | Poor | Depends on linkage |

Practical Considerations:

  1. Feature scaling: Standardize features so distances are comparable (see Linear Algebra and Geometry)

  2. Distance metric: - Euclidean: Default for continuous features - Manhattan: For mixed feature types - Cosine: For high-dimensional sparse data

  3. Spatial indexing: Use R*-tree or KD-tree for large datasets

  4. Validation: - Silhouette score - Davies-Bouldin index - Visual inspection

  5. Handling varying densities: - Use HDBSCAN instead - Apply DBSCAN to subsets with similar density - Use local density estimates

Example Application - Anomaly Detection:

DBSCAN naturally identifies outliers, making it useful for anomaly detection:

  1. Run DBSCAN on data 2. Points marked as noise are potential anomalies 3. Can combine with other methods (see Supervised Learning for classification approaches)

Connection to Graph Theory:

DBSCAN can be viewed as finding connected components in a graph: - Nodes: Data points - Edges: Connect points within \epsilon distance - Core points: Nodes with degree \geq MinPts - Clusters: Connected components of core points

This perspective connects to spectral clustering (see Section 1.1) and graph-based methods.

Applications:

  • Spatial data analysis (GIS) - Anomaly detection in network traffic - Image segmentation - Customer segmentation with outlier detection - Astronomical data analysis - Trajectory clustering

DBSCAN provides a powerful alternative to centroid-based methods when clusters have complex shapes or when outlier detection is important (James et al. 2023; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014).

Gaussian Mixture Models (GMMs)

Gaussian Mixture Models provide a probabilistic approach to clustering by modeling the data as a mixture of multiple Gaussian distributions. Unlike K-means (see Section 1.1) which performs hard assignment, GMMs provide soft assignments through posterior probabilities, making them more flexible and statistically principled.

Model Definition: A GMM represents the data distribution as a weighted sum of K Gaussian components:

p(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)

where: - \pi_k are mixing coefficients (weights) with \pi_k \geq 0 and \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k = 1 - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k \in \mathbb{R}^d is the mean of component k - \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d} is the covariance matrix of component k - \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}|\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}) is the multivariate Gaussian density (see Probability and Statistics)

Latent Variable Interpretation: Introduce latent variable z_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\} indicating which component generated \mathbf{x}_i:

p(z_i = k) = \pi_k p(\mathbf{x}_i | z_i = k) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i | \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)

The marginal distribution is:

p(\mathbf{x}_i) = \sum_{k=1}^K p(z_i = k) p(\mathbf{x}_i | z_i = k) = \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i | \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)

Maximum Likelihood Estimation: Given data \mathcal{X} = \{\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n\}, maximize the log-likelihood:

\log p(\mathcal{X} | \boldsymbol{\theta}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log \left(\sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i | \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)\right)

where \boldsymbol{\theta} = \{\pi_k, \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k\}_{k=1}^K are the parameters.

Direct optimization is difficult due to the sum inside the logarithm. The Expectation-Maximization (EM) algorithm provides an iterative solution.

Expectation-Maximization (EM) Algorithm:

EM alternates between computing the expected complete-data log-likelihood (E-step) and maximizing it (M-step).

E-Step (Expectation): Compute the posterior probability (responsibility) that component k generated point \mathbf{x}_i:

\gamma_{ik} = p(z_i = k | \mathbf{x}_i, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}) = \frac{\pi_k^{(t)} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i | \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t)}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{(t)})}{\sum_{j=1}^K \pi_j^{(t)} \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i | \boldsymbol{\mu}_j^{(t)}, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_j^{(t)})}

This follows from Bayes’ theorem (see Probability and Statistics):

p(z_i = k | \mathbf{x}_i) = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_i | z_i = k) p(z_i = k)}{p(\mathbf{x}_i)} = \frac{p(\mathbf{x}_i | z_i = k) p(z_i = k)}{\sum_{j=1}^K p(\mathbf{x}_i | z_i = j) p(z_i = j)}

The responsibilities satisfy \sum_{k=1}^K \gamma_{ik} = 1 for each i.

M-Step (Maximization): Update parameters to maximize the expected complete-data log-likelihood:

Effective number of points assigned to component k: N_k = \sum_{i=1}^n \gamma_{ik}

Update mixing coefficients: \pi_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{N_k}{n}

Update means: \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^n \gamma_{ik} \mathbf{x}_i

Update covariances: \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{(t+1)} = \frac{1}{N_k} \sum_{i=1}^n \gamma_{ik} (\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)})(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k^{(t+1)})^T

Derivation of M-Step Updates: The M-step maximizes:

Q(\boldsymbol{\theta}, \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)}) = \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^K \gamma_{ik} \log[\pi_k \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_i | \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k)]

For \boldsymbol{\mu}_k, taking the derivative and setting to zero:

\frac{\partial Q}{\partial \boldsymbol{\mu}_k} = \sum_{i=1}^n \gamma_{ik} \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}_k) = 0

Solving: \boldsymbol{\mu}_k = \frac{\sum_i \gamma_{ik} \mathbf{x}_i}{\sum_i \gamma_{ik}}

Similar derivations yield the updates for \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k and \pi_k (with Lagrange multiplier for the constraint \sum_k \pi_k = 1).

Complete EM Algorithm:

``` Input: Data X = {x₁, …, xₙ}, number of components K Initialize: πₖ, μₖ, Σₖ for k = 1, …, K

Repeat until convergence: # E-Step For i = 1 to n: For k = 1 to K: γᵢₖ = πₖ N(xᵢ|μₖ,Σₖ) / Σⱼ πⱼ N(xᵢ|μⱼ,Σⱼ)

# M-Step For k = 1 to K: Nₖ = Σᵢ γᵢₖ πₖ = Nₖ / n μₖ = (Σᵢ γᵢₖ xᵢ) / Nₖ Σₖ = (Σᵢ γᵢₖ (xᵢ-μₖ)(xᵢ-μₖ)ᵀ) / Nₖ

# Check convergence If change in log-likelihood < threshold: Break

Return: πₖ, μₖ, Σₖ ```

Convergence Properties:

  1. Monotonic increase: EM guarantees that the log-likelihood increases (or stays the same) at each iteration: \log p(\mathcal{X} | \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t+1)}) \geq \log p(\mathcal{X} | \boldsymbol{\theta}^{(t)})

  2. Local maximum: EM converges to a local maximum of the likelihood, not necessarily global

  3. Convergence criterion: Stop when: - Change in log-likelihood < \epsilon

  • Change in parameters < \epsilon
  • Maximum iterations reached

Initialization Strategies:

  1. K-means initialization: Run K-means first, use cluster centers as initial means 2. Random initialization: Randomly select K data points as initial means 3. Multiple random starts: Run EM multiple times with different initializations, keep best result 4. Hierarchical clustering: Use hierarchical clustering to initialize

Covariance Matrix Constraints:

To reduce parameters and improve stability:

  1. Full covariance: \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k is unconstrained (d(d+1)/2 parameters per component) 2. Diagonal covariance: \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k = \text{diag}(\sigma_{k1}^2, \ldots, \sigma_{kd}^2) (d parameters) 3. Spherical covariance: \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k = \sigma_k^2 \mathbf{I} (1 parameter) 4. Tied covariance: All components share the same \boldsymbol{\Sigma}

Model Selection: Choose the number of components K:

1. Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC): \text{BIC} = -2\log p(\mathcal{X} | \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) + p \log n

where p is the number of free parameters: - Full covariance: p = K(1 + d + d(d+1)/2) - 1 - Diagonal: p = K(1 + 2d) - 1 - Spherical: p = K(1 + d + 1) - 1

Choose K that minimizes BIC.

2. Akaike Information Criterion (AIC): \text{AIC} = -2\log p(\mathcal{X} | \hat{\boldsymbol{\theta}}) + 2p

AIC tends to favor more complex models than BIC.

3. Cross-Validation: Evaluate held-out log-likelihood

4. Silhouette Score: Assign points to most likely component, compute silhouette

Relationship to K-Means:

K-means is a special case of GMM: - Set all \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k = \sigma^2 \mathbf{I} with \sigma \to 0 - Hard assignments: \gamma_{ik} \in \{0, 1\} - K-means objective emerges as limiting case

GMM generalizes K-means by: - Allowing elliptical clusters (full covariance) - Providing soft assignments (probabilities) - Having probabilistic interpretation

Advantages of GMMs:

  1. Probabilistic framework: Provides uncertainty estimates 2. Soft clustering: Points can belong to multiple clusters 3. Flexible cluster shapes: Can model elliptical clusters 4. Generative model: Can sample new data points 5. Principled model selection: Use BIC/AIC 6. Density estimation: Models full data distribution

Disadvantages:

  1. Computational cost: O(nKd^2T) where T is number of EM iterations 2. Local optima: Sensitive to initialization 3. Number of components: Must specify K
  2. Singularities: Covariance matrices can become singular 5. Overfitting: Can overfit with too many components 6. Assumes Gaussian: May not fit non-Gaussian data well

Regularization Techniques:

  1. Add small constant to diagonal: \boldsymbol{\Sigma}_k + \epsilon \mathbf{I} to prevent singularities 2. Minimum covariance: Enforce minimum eigenvalues 3. Bayesian GMM: Place priors on parameters (Dirichlet process mixture models) 4. Constrain covariance: Use diagonal or spherical covariances

Extensions:

  1. Variational Bayesian GMM: Bayesian treatment with variational inference 2. Dirichlet Process Mixture Models: Infinite mixture models 3. Student’s t-Mixture Models: More robust to outliers 4. Factor Analysis: GMM with low-rank covariance structure (see Section 1.3)

Applications:

  • Image segmentation - Speaker recognition (GMM-UBM) - Anomaly detection - Density estimation - Background subtraction in video - Bioinformatics (gene expression clustering)

Connection to Other Methods:

  • K-means: Special case with spherical, equal-variance components - PCA: Related through factor analysis (see
    • Hidden Markov Models: GMMs for emission distributions - Variational Autoencoders: Can use GMM prior (see Section 1.3)

GMMs provide a flexible, probabilistic framework for clustering and density estimation, bridging the gap between simple methods like K-means and more complex generative models (Bishop and Bishop 2024; Deisenroth et al. 2020).

Spectral Clustering

Algorithm: 1. Construct similarity graph with weights w_{ij} = \exp(-\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2/(2\sigma^2)) 2. Compute graph Laplacian: \mathbf{L} = \mathbf{D} - \mathbf{W} where D_{ii} = \sum_j w_{ij} 3. Compute first K eigenvectors of \mathbf{L} 4. Apply K-means to rows of eigenvector matrix

Normalized Spectral Clustering: Use normalized Laplacian \mathbf{L}_{\text{norm}} = \mathbf{D}^{-1/2}\mathbf{L}\mathbf{D}^{-1/2}.

Advantages: Can find non-convex clusters by working in spectral space (James et al. 2023; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014).

Dimensionality Reduction

Dimensionality reduction finds low-dimensional representations of high-dimensional data.

For more on this topic, see Bishop and Bishop (2024, Ch. 16), James et al. (2023, Ch. 12) and Theodoridis (2026, Ch. 20).

Principal Component Analysis (PCA)

Principal Component Analysis (PCA) is a fundamental technique for dimensionality reduction that finds a low-dimensional representation of data that captures most of the variance. PCA identifies orthogonal directions (principal components) along which the data varies most, providing both a method for visualization and a preprocessing step for other algorithms.

Problem Setup: Given data \mathbf{X} = [\mathbf{x}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{x}_n]^T \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d} where each \mathbf{x}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d, find a lower-dimensional representation \mathbf{Z} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times k} with k < d that preserves as much information as possible.

Two Equivalent Formulations:

1. Maximum Variance Formulation: Find directions that maximize the variance of projected data.

For the first principal component, solve:

\max_{\mathbf{w}_1: \|\mathbf{w}_1\|=1} \text{Var}(\mathbf{X}\mathbf{w}_1) = \max_{\mathbf{w}_1: \|\mathbf{w}_1\|=1} \mathbf{w}_1^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_1

where \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n (\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})^T is the sample covariance matrix and \bar{\mathbf{x}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{x}_i is the sample mean.

Solution via Lagrange Multipliers: Form the Lagrangian:

\mathcal{L}(\mathbf{w}_1, \lambda) = \mathbf{w}_1^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_1 - \lambda(\mathbf{w}_1^T\mathbf{w}_1 - 1)

Taking the derivative and setting to zero:

\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \mathbf{w}_1} = 2\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_1 - 2\lambda\mathbf{w}_1 = 0

This gives the eigenvalue equation:

\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_1 = \lambda\mathbf{w}_1

The variance along \mathbf{w}_1 is:

\mathbf{w}_1^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_1 = \mathbf{w}_1^T(\lambda\mathbf{w}_1) = \lambda\mathbf{w}_1^T\mathbf{w}_1 = \lambda

To maximize variance, choose \mathbf{w}_1 as the eigenvector corresponding to the largest eigenvalue \lambda_1.

For subsequent components, maximize variance subject to orthogonality:

\max_{\mathbf{w}_j: \|\mathbf{w}_j\|=1, \mathbf{w}_j \perp \mathbf{w}_i \forall i<j} \mathbf{w}_j^T\mathbf{S}\mathbf{w}_j

The solution is the eigenvector corresponding to the j-th largest eigenvalue.

2. Minimum Reconstruction Error Formulation: Find a k-dimensional subspace that minimizes reconstruction error.

\min_{\mathbf{W}_k, \mathbf{Z}} \sum_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}} - \mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{z}_i\|^2

where \mathbf{W}_k \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times k} has orthonormal columns and \mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^k.

Solution: The optimal \mathbf{W}_k consists of the first k eigenvectors of \mathbf{S}, and:

\mathbf{z}_i = \mathbf{W}_k^T(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}})

Equivalence: These two formulations are equivalent. Maximizing variance in the projection is equivalent to minimizing reconstruction error.

Algorithm (PCA via Eigendecomposition):

  1. Center the data: \tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^T where \mathbf{1} is a vector of ones

  2. Compute covariance matrix: \mathbf{S} = \frac{1}{n}\tilde{\mathbf{X}}^T\tilde{\mathbf{X}}

  3. Eigendecomposition: Compute eigenvalues and eigenvectors: \mathbf{S} = \mathbf{W}\boldsymbol{\Lambda}\mathbf{W}^T where \boldsymbol{\Lambda} = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_d) with \lambda_1 \geq \lambda_2 \geq \cdots \geq \lambda_d \geq 0

  4. Select components: Choose first k eigenvectors: \mathbf{W}_k = [\mathbf{w}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{w}_k]

  5. Project data: \mathbf{Z} = \tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{W}_k

Algorithm (PCA via SVD - Preferred):

For numerical stability and efficiency, use Singular Value Decomposition:

  1. Center the data: \tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{X} - \mathbf{1}\bar{\mathbf{x}}^T

  2. SVD: Compute \tilde{\mathbf{X}} = \mathbf{U}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}\mathbf{V}^T

  • \mathbf{U} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}: Left singular vectors - \boldsymbol{\Sigma} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times d}: Singular values (diagonal) - \mathbf{V} \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times d}: Right singular vectors (principal components)

  1. Principal components: \mathbf{W}_k = \mathbf{V}_{:,1:k} (first k columns of \mathbf{V})

  2. Eigenvalues: \lambda_j = \sigma_j^2 / n where \sigma_j are singular values

  3. Project data: \mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{U}_{:,1:k}\boldsymbol{\Sigma}_{1:k,1:k} = \tilde{\mathbf{X}}\mathbf{W}_k

Advantages of SVD: - More numerically stable - Avoids forming \mathbf{S} explicitly (saves memory) - Faster for n \ll d or d \ll n - Directly provides principal components

Dimensionality Reduction and Reconstruction:

Projection (Encoding): \mathbf{z}_i = \mathbf{W}_k^T(\mathbf{x}_i - \bar{\mathbf{x}}) \in \mathbb{R}^k

Reconstruction (Decoding): \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i = \bar{\mathbf{x}} + \mathbf{W}_k\mathbf{z}_i \in \mathbb{R}^d

Reconstruction Error: \text{Error} = \sum_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{x}_i - \tilde{\mathbf{x}}_i\|^2 = \sum_{j=k+1}^d \lambda_j

The reconstruction error equals the sum of discarded eigenvalues.

Choosing the Number of Components k:

  1. Proportion of Variance Explained (PVE): \text{PVE}(k) = \frac{\sum_{j=1}^k \lambda_j}{\sum_{j=1}^d \lambda_j}

    Choose k such that PVE(k) \geq 0.9 (or 0.95, 0.99)

  2. Scree Plot: Plot eigenvalues vs. component number and look for an “elbow”

  3. Kaiser Criterion: Keep components with \lambda_j > \bar{\lambda} (average eigenvalue)

  4. Cross-Validation: Use downstream task performance

  5. Parallel Analysis: Compare eigenvalues to those from random data

Geometric Interpretation:

  • PCA finds the best-fitting k-dimensional hyperplane through the data - Principal components are orthogonal axes of this hyperplane - First PC points in direction of maximum variance - Each subsequent PC is orthogonal and captures maximum remaining variance - Data points are projected onto this hyperplane

Properties of PCA:

  1. Orthogonality: Principal components are orthogonal: \mathbf{w}_i^T\mathbf{w}_j = \delta_{ij}

  2. Uncorrelated Projections: Projected data has diagonal covariance: \text{Cov}(\mathbf{Z}) = \boldsymbol{\Lambda}_k = \text{diag}(\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_k)

  3. Optimal Linear Projection: PCA is optimal among all linear projections for: - Maximizing variance - Minimizing reconstruction error

  4. Rotation Invariant: PCA is invariant to rotations of the coordinate system

  5. Not Scale Invariant: Sensitive to feature scales (requires standardization)

Standardization: For features with different scales, standardize first:

\mathbf{x}_i' = \frac{\mathbf{x}_i - \boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}

where \boldsymbol{\mu} and \boldsymbol{\sigma} are feature-wise means and standard deviations. This is equivalent to PCA on the correlation matrix instead of covariance matrix.

Computational Complexity:

  • Covariance matrix: O(nd^2)
  • Eigendecomposition: O(d^3)
  • SVD: O(\min(nd^2, n^2d))
  • Total: O(nd^2 + d^3) for eigendecomposition, O(\min(nd^2, n^2d)) for SVD - Projection: O(ndk) for projecting n points

Kernel PCA (Nonlinear Extension):

For nonlinear dimensionality reduction, apply PCA in a high-dimensional feature space \phi(\mathbf{x}) using the kernel trick.

Kernel Matrix: \mathbf{K}_{ij} = k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j) = \phi(\mathbf{x}_i)^T\phi(\mathbf{x}_j)

Centering: Center the kernel matrix: \tilde{\mathbf{K}} = \mathbf{K} - \mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{K} - \mathbf{K}\mathbf{1}_n + \mathbf{1}_n\mathbf{K}\mathbf{1}_n

where \mathbf{1}_n = \frac{1}{n}\mathbf{1}\mathbf{1}^T.

Eigendecomposition: Solve \tilde{\mathbf{K}}\boldsymbol{\alpha} = \lambda\boldsymbol{\alpha}

Projection: For a new point \mathbf{x}: z_j = \sum_{i=1}^n \alpha_{ij} k(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x})

Common Kernels: - RBF: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = \exp(-\gamma\|\mathbf{x} - \mathbf{x}'\|^2) - Polynomial: k(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{x}') = (\mathbf{x}^T\mathbf{x}' + c)^p

Applications of PCA:

  1. Visualization: Project high-dimensional data to 2D or 3D 2. Noise reduction: Remove components with small eigenvalues
  2. Feature extraction: Create uncorrelated features 4. Preprocessing: Reduce dimensionality before applying other algorithms 5. Compression: Reduce storage requirements 6. Anomaly detection: Points far from the principal subspace are anomalies 7. Face recognition: Eigenfaces for face representation

Limitations:

  1. Linearity: Only captures linear relationships 2. Global Method: Finds global structure, may miss local patterns 3. Variance ≠ Information: High variance directions may not be most informative 4. Interpretability: Principal components are linear combinations, hard to interpret 5. Outlier Sensitivity: Sensitive to outliers (use robust PCA variants) 6. Assumes Gaussian: Implicitly assumes data is Gaussian-distributed

Practical Tips:

  1. Always center data: Essential for PCA 2. Standardize features: When features have different scales 3. Check for outliers: Remove or use robust PCA 4. Visualize: Plot first 2-3 components 5. Validate: Check if downstream tasks improve 6. Consider alternatives: t-SNE, UMAP for visualization; autoencoders for nonlinear reduction

PCA remains one of the most fundamental and widely used techniques in machine learning, providing a principled approach to dimensionality reduction with strong theoretical foundations (James et al. 2023; Deisenroth et al. 2020).

Multidimensional Scaling (MDS)

Classical MDS: Find configuration preserving pairwise distances.

Given distance matrix \mathbf{D} with D_{ij} = d(\mathbf{x}_i, \mathbf{x}_j), find \mathbf{z}_1, \ldots, \mathbf{z}_n \in \mathbb{R}^k minimizing:

\text{Stress} = \sqrt{\sum_{i<j}(d_{ij} - \|\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_j\|)^2}

Metric MDS: Preserve distances exactly (when possible).

Non-metric MDS: Preserve rank order of distances.

Manifold Learning

Isomap (Isometric Mapping): 1. Construct k-nearest neighbor graph 2. Compute geodesic distances using shortest paths 3. Apply classical MDS to geodesic distance matrix

Locally Linear Embedding (LLE): 1. Find k nearest neighbors for each point 2. Compute reconstruction weights: \min_{\mathbf{W}} \sum_i \|\mathbf{x}_i - \sum_j W_{ij}\mathbf{x}_j\|^2 3. Find low-dimensional embedding preserving weights: \min_{\mathbf{Z}} \sum_i \|\mathbf{z}_i - \sum_j W_{ij}\mathbf{z}_j\|^2

t-SNE (t-Distributed Stochastic Neighbor Embedding):

Define similarity in high-dimensional space:

p_{j|i} = \frac{\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2/(2\sigma_i^2))}{\sum_{k \neq i}\exp(-\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_k\|^2/(2\sigma_i^2))}

Define similarity in low-dimensional space using t-distribution:

q_{ij} = \frac{(1 + \|\mathbf{z}_i - \mathbf{z}_j\|^2)^{-1}}{\sum_{k \neq l}(1 + \|\mathbf{z}_k - \mathbf{z}_l\|^2)^{-1}}

Minimize KL divergence: \text{KL}(P||Q) = \sum_{i,j} p_{ij}\log(p_{ij}/q_{ij})

UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection): Based on Riemannian geometry and algebraic topology. Often faster than t-SNE with better global structure preservation.

Random Projections

Johnson-Lindenstrauss Lemma: For any \epsilon \in (0,1) and set of n points in \mathbb{R}^d, there exists a linear map f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^k with k = O(\epsilon^{-2}\log n) such that:

(1-\epsilon)\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2 \leq \|f(\mathbf{x}_i) - f(\mathbf{x}_j)\|^2 \leq (1+\epsilon)\|\mathbf{x}_i - \mathbf{x}_j\|^2

Random Projection Matrix: Sample entries from \mathcal{N}(0, 1/k) or \{-1/\sqrt{k}, +1/\sqrt{k}\}.

Applications: Fast approximate nearest neighbor search, compressed sensing (James et al. 2023; Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David 2014).

Generative Models and Latent Variables

Generative models learn the data distribution p(\mathbf{x}) and can generate new samples.

For more on this topic, see Deisenroth et al. (2020, Ch. 11), James et al. (2023, Ch. 4) and Shalev-Shwartz and Ben-David (2014, Ch. 24).

Latent Variable Models

General Framework: Introduce latent variables \mathbf{z}:

p(\mathbf{x}) = \int p(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})p(\mathbf{z})d\mathbf{z}

Factor Analysis

Model:

\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{z} + \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\epsilon}

where \mathbf{z} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}) and \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \boldsymbol{\Psi}) with \boldsymbol{\Psi} diagonal.

Marginal Distribution:

p(\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{W}\mathbf{W}^T + \boldsymbol{\Psi})

Estimation: Use EM algorithm or variational methods.

Difference from PCA: Factor analysis is probabilistic and models observation noise.

Autoencoders

Architecture: Encoder-decoder network: - Encoder: \mathbf{z} = f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{enc}}) - Decoder: \hat{\mathbf{x}} = f_{\text{dec}}(\mathbf{z}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_{\text{dec}})

Objective: Minimize reconstruction error:

L = \sum_{i=1}^n \|\mathbf{x}_i - f_{\text{dec}}(f_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x}_i))\|^2

Variants: - Sparse autoencoders: Add sparsity penalty on \mathbf{z} - Denoising autoencoders: Reconstruct from corrupted input - Contractive autoencoders: Penalize sensitivity to input perturbations

Variational Autoencoders (VAEs)

Probabilistic Formulation: - Prior: p(\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}) - Decoder: p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{z}), \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}^2(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{I}) - Encoder (recognition model): q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) = \mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{x}), \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}^2(\mathbf{x})\mathbf{I})

Evidence Lower Bound (ELBO):

\log p(\mathbf{x}) \geq \mathbb{E}_{q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x})}[\log p_{\boldsymbol{\theta}}(\mathbf{x}|\mathbf{z})] - D_{\text{KL}}(q_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{z}|\mathbf{x}) \| p(\mathbf{z}))

Reparameterization Trick: Sample \mathbf{z} = \boldsymbol{\mu}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{x}) + \boldsymbol{\sigma}_{\boldsymbol{\phi}}(\mathbf{x}) \odot \boldsymbol{\epsilon} where \boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I}).

Generative Adversarial Networks (GANs)

Framework: Two networks compete: - Generator: G(\mathbf{z}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_G) maps noise to data - Discriminator: D(\mathbf{x}; \boldsymbol{\theta}_D) distinguishes real from fake

Objective (Minimax Game):

\min_G \max_D V(D, G) = \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{x} \sim p_{\text{data}}}[\log D(\mathbf{x})] + \mathbb{E}_{\mathbf{z} \sim p(\mathbf{z})}[\log(1 - D(G(\mathbf{z})))]

Training: 1. Update D to maximize V(D, G) 2. Update G to minimize V(D, G)

Challenges: - Mode collapse: Generator produces limited variety - Training instability: Difficult to balance D and G

Variants: - DCGAN: Deep convolutional architecture - Wasserstein GAN: Uses Wasserstein distance - StyleGAN: Disentangled style-based generation - Conditional GAN: Condition on labels or other information

Normalizing Flows

Idea: Transform simple distribution through invertible mappings.

Given invertible f: \mathbb{R}^d \to \mathbb{R}^d:

p_{\mathbf{x}}(\mathbf{x}) = p_{\mathbf{z}}(f^{-1}(\mathbf{x}))\left|\det\frac{\partial f^{-1}}{\partial \mathbf{x}}\right|

Composition: Chain multiple transformations:

\mathbf{x} = f_K \circ f_{K-1} \circ \cdots \circ f_1(\mathbf{z})

Requirements: - Easy to compute f and f^{-1} - Easy to compute Jacobian determinant

Examples: - RealNVP: Affine coupling layers - Glow: Invertible 1×1 convolutions - Neural Spline Flows: Monotonic spline transformations

Advantages: Exact likelihood computation, stable training (Bishop and Bishop 2024; Theodoridis 2026).
