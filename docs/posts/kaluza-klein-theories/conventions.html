Prerequisites and conventions

Who these lectures are for

The reader is assumed to know calculus of one and several variables and linear algebra at the level of a first university course, and to have met Newtonian mechanics. Nothing else is assumed: the foundations of mathematics (Part I) develop set theory and topology, linear algebra, real and complex analysis, differential equations, Fourier analysis, Hilbert spaces and operator theory, probability, differential geometry, Lie groups and their representations, coset spaces and fibre bundles on explicit examples, and the foundations of physics (Part II) develop, in this order, classical mechanics, classical electromagnetism, thermodynamics and statistical mechanics, quantum mechanics, classical field theory, the Dirac equation, quantum field theory, gauge theories and symmetry breaking, renormalisation, special and general relativity, supersymmetry and supergravity, each from its definitions. A reader who knows a chapter can skim its definitions and try its exercises.

How the verification works

Every claim of the book, in a definition, a theorem, an example or the answer to an exercise, is a theorem of a Lean library built on mathlib (The mathlib Community 2020; Moura and Ullrich 2021). The computational core of each proof is a Veriphy certificate (Douzas 2026): SageMath (The Sage Developers 2026) finds the data of the certificate (coefficients, witnesses, closed forms, eigenvectors), the Lean kernel checks the proof built from it, and only the Sage-free proof is committed, so the library builds without Sage and contains no sorry. Physical laws are formalised as explicit mathematical models (states and observables on Hilbert spaces, ensembles as probability measures, time-sliced path integrals, regularised integrals and their flow equations) and the book’s statements are theorems of those models.

The verification never interrupts the text. Below a block, a collapsed box labelled Verification opens like the solution of an exercise. It always has the same shape: a Veriphy line names the recipe and the certificate, and the certificate splits into its two halves, the Lean statement that the kernel checks and the Sage computation that found the certificate’s data. For the symmetry of the Christoffel symbols it reads:

TipVerification

Veriphy. Recipe calc_derivation; certificate christoffel_symm.

Lean.

/-- For a symmetric metric the Christoffel symbols are symmetric in the lower indices. -/
theorem christoffel_symm {n : ℕ} (g : Fin n → Fin n → (Fin n → ℝ) → ℝ) (hg : ∀ i j, g i j = g j i)
    (k i j : Fin n) (x : Fin n → ℝ) :
    christoffel g k i j x = christoffel g k j i x

KaluzaKlein.christoffel_symm in KaluzaKlein/Conventions.lean, namespace KaluzaKlein

Sage. Pending.

The Lean part reproduces the docstring and the statement of the theorem, extracted from the Lean source when the book is rendered. A part marked Pending belongs to a claim whose certificate is still being produced. Registry of verified results lists every claim with its Lean theorem and its recipe.

Reading the blocks

Every numbered block of the book is one of three kinds, told apart by the colour of its title. Statements, that is, definitions, theorems, lemmas, propositions and corollaries, have a red title and stand in a light red box, which also holds the statement’s collapsed Verification box; each definition introduces exactly one notion. Examples have a green title; every statement is followed by a run of them, of increasing complexity, which carry the figures and their interactive versions. Exercises have a blue title; every statement is followed by a run of them, of increasing difficulty, each with a folded solution, and every chapter ends with exercises that draw on all of its sections. To keep the line of the argument visible, the examples of a statement are folded into one collapsed Examples box placed right after it, and its exercises, with their solutions, into one collapsed Exercises box; open them to work through the material. Solutions that use Sage show the committed script and its recorded output.

Coordinates, indices and signature

Spacetime is four-dimensional with coordinates x^\mu, \mu = 0, 1, 2, 3, and metric signature (+, -, -, -): \eta_{\mu\nu} = \operatorname{diag}(1, -1, -1, -1), timelike vectors have \eta(v, v) > 0 and spacelike ones \eta(v, v) < 0. Internal (compact) coordinates are y^m, and the full higher-dimensional coordinates are z^M = (x^\mu, y^m). Internal metrics are negative definite, so that the full metric \hat g_{MN} has signature (+, -, \dots, -): the Kaluza-Klein ansatz reads d\hat s^2 = g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu - \phi^2(dy + A_\mu dx^\mu)^2 on the circle and g_{\mu\nu}dx^\mu dx^\nu - R^2(d\theta^2 + \sin^2\theta\,d\varphi^2) on the sphere, and we write h_{mn} = -\gamma_{mn} with \gamma_{mn} the positive-definite round metric when the internal geometry is discussed on its own (Part I treats Riemannian metrics, which are positive definite). Indices are summed when repeated once up and once down.

The signature fixes the signs of the field-theory formulas: the Klein-Gordon equation is (\Box + m^2)\phi = 0 with \Box = \eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\partial_\nu = \partial_t^2 - \nabla^2, the scalar Lagrangian is \tfrac12\eta^{\mu\nu}\partial_\mu\phi\,\partial_\nu\phi - V(\phi), the Maxwell and Yang-Mills Lagrangians are -\tfrac14F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} (unchanged by the signature, two indices being raised), the Dirac equation is (i\gamma^\mu\partial_\mu - m)\psi = 0 with \{\gamma^\mu, \gamma^\nu\} = 2\eta^{\mu\nu}, (\gamma^0)^2 = 1, (\gamma^i)^2 = -1, and the Einstein-Hilbert Lagrangian is -\tfrac{1}{16\pi G}\sqrt{-g}\,R with the curvature conventions of Section 1.5 (the curvature scalar changes sign with the signature while the Riemann and Ricci tensors do not). Under the ansatz above the circle reduction gives \hat R = R + \tfrac14\phi^2 F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} - 2\phi^{-1}\Box\phi (Part III).

The Kaluza-Klein literature is split on the signature: Overduin and Wesson (1997, sec. 3), Tanii (1998) and the CSDR reviews (Manolakos and Zoupanos 2016, eq. 9; Manousselis and Zoupanos 2000) use (+,-,-,-) as we do, while Duff (1994, 2), Duff et al. (2025) and Martin (1997) use (-,+,+,+). Where we quote a formula from a source with the other signature, we say so and convert: the rule is g_{MN} \to -g_{MN}, which leaves \Gamma, R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu}, R_{\mu\nu} and F_{\mu\nu}F^{\mu\nu} unchanged and flips the signs of R, of \Box, of every \eta^{\mu\nu} contraction with an odd number of inverse metrics, and of the \eta in the Clifford relation.

In the Lean library a chart is \mathbb{R}^n, written Fin n → ℝ, and a metric is the family of its components g_{ij}(x). The chart hypotheses (for instance \sin\theta \neq 0 on the sphere) exclude the points where the coordinates degenerate; every statement holds on the chart domain.

Connection and curvature

The Levi-Civita connection has Christoffel symbols \Gamma^k_{ij} = \tfrac12 g^{kl} \left( \partial_i g_{lj} + \partial_j g_{li} - \partial_l g_{ij} \right), \tag{1} the Riemann tensor is R^\rho{}_{\sigma\mu\nu} = \partial_\mu \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\sigma} - \partial_\nu \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\sigma} + \Gamma^\rho_{\mu\lambda} \Gamma^\lambda_{\nu\sigma} - \Gamma^\rho_{\nu\lambda} \Gamma^\lambda_{\mu\sigma}, \tag{2} the Ricci tensor is R_{\sigma\nu} = R^\rho{}_{\sigma\rho\nu} and the scalar curvature is R = g^{\sigma\nu} R_{\sigma\nu}. With these signs the round unit sphere has R = +2 and the Einstein-Hilbert action of general relativity is S = \frac{1}{2\kappa^2} \int d^4x \sqrt{-g}\, R with \kappa^2 = 8\pi G. These are the definitions coded in the Lean library (CoordinateGeometry.christoffel, riemann, ricci, scalarCurvature), so every curvature statement is verified with exactly these signs.

Lie algebras and gauge fields

Lie algebra generators are anti-Hermitian matrices T_A with real structure constants, [T_A, T_B] = f_{AB}{}^C\, T_C . \tag{3} For \mathfrak{so}(3) \cong \mathfrak{su}(2) the generators are the real matrices (L_a)_{bc} = -\epsilon_{abc}, or equivalently T_a = -\tfrac{i}{2}\sigma_a with the Pauli matrices, and f_{ab}{}^c = \epsilon_{abc}. This is the convention of the CSDR literature (Kubyshin 2003, sec. 1; Chatzistavrakidis et al. 2007, eq. 2.4), in which the generators of a coset S/R are split into Q_i spanning \mathfrak{r} and Q_a spanning its complement.

A gauge field is a Lie-algebra-valued one-form A = A_M\, dz^M with anti-Hermitian components, the covariant derivative is D_M = \partial_M + A_M, and the field strength is F_{MN} = \partial_M A_N - \partial_N A_M + [A_M, A_N], \qquad F = dA + A \wedge A . \tag{4} The Yang-Mills action is S = \frac{1}{2 g^2} \int d^Dz \sqrt{|g|}\, \operatorname{Tr}(F_{MN} F^{MN}) with the trace in the fundamental representation and a positive coupling g. With F_{MN} = F^a_{MN}T_a and \operatorname{Tr}(T_aT_b) = -\tfrac12\delta_{ab} this is S = -\frac{1}{4g^2}\int d^Dz\sqrt{|g|}\,F^a_{MN}F^{a\,MN}, whose electric part \tfrac{1}{2g^2}F^a_{0i}F^a_{0i} enters with the positive sign of a kinetic energy in the signature (+, -, \dots, -). Manolakos and Zoupanos (2016) and Manousselis and Zoupanos (2000) write F_{MN} = \partial_M A_N - \partial_N A_M - [A_M, A_N] with D_M = \partial_M - A_M, which is the same convention after A \to -A; Kubyshin (2003) prints the commutator with a minus sign in his eq. (9) but uses F = dA + A\wedge A in his example (see the reading note). We never need a gauge coupling inside the internal space, since CSDR fixes the internal components of A in units of the coset radius.

Units

\hbar = c = 1. The five-dimensional gravitational coupling is \hat\kappa^2 = 8\pi \hat G; the circle has circumference 2\pi R. Lie algebra generators are normalised by \operatorname{Tr}(T_a T_b) = -\tfrac12 \delta_{ab} in the fundamental representation of SU(2) and SU(3).
